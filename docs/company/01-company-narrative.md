# Company narrative

Not a pitch. The strategic story the founders can tell consistently to each other, to
houses, to hires, to partners and later to investors. Every claim is either from the
founding pack, from the repository, or from a cited source; hypotheses are named as such.

## Problem

Egyptians who want a local perfume find it through scattered social pages, shops and
sites, with inconsistent descriptions and no way to compare or verify (doc 01 §2). The
houses that make those perfumes take orders through DMs, ship by hand and reconcile cash
themselves; they cannot prove their reliability to a stranger, and they duplicate cost on
acquisition and delivery (doc 01 §2). Trust attaches to individual pages, not to the
category.

## Why now (HYPOTHESES with cited context)

Egypt's fragrance category is growing in value even under inflation, with mass and local
brands the most dynamic segment (Euromonitor summary, cited in `docs/market/01`). Social
commerce is where discovery happens (doc 02 §1; press lists of local houses show Instagram
as their channel). COD dominates and fails often (cited 20–35 % first-attempt failure),
which punishes houses without operations. Data protection has just become enforceable
(PDPL executive regulations, Nov 2025), rewarding a compliant platform. None of this is
proof the marketplace will win (doc 02 research rule); it is the reason to test now.

## Market context

Generic marketplaces list fragrance broadly and publish category commissions; imported
"original" perfume e-tailers compete on authenticity and discounts; local houses are
discovered through press and social; no verified specialist local-perfume marketplace was
observed in the scan (`docs/market/`). We do not claim to be first.

## Product

A responsive Arabic-first marketplace of verified Egyptian perfume houses, each with its
own storefront, and a seller operating system behind it: listings in a structured scent
schema, order queue with SLA clocks, courier and COD handling, statements with itemised
fees, and attribution that charges a low fee on a house's own customers and a marketplace
rate only where the platform brought the buyer. Today (FACT): the shopfront, portal and
console exist with strong foundations and six money-path defects to fix before any real
order (`docs/product/01-mvp-readiness.md`).

## Value proposition

Customers: compare local perfumes on the same terms, buy from checked houses, read reviews
from delivered orders, see every cost before paying, deal with one relationship. Houses:
a storefront and operations in a day, fair economics proven per order, money they can
check, incremental demand, a reputation they can carry (`docs/product-strategy/04`).

## Business model

Hybrid marketplace plus seller OS (doc 03). Seller-originated orders: 3–5 % or a
subscription alternative; marketplace-generated: 12–18 % of completed net merchandise;
later sponsored visibility, subscriptions, fulfilment. Commission only on completed orders.
The asymmetry is the model's heart and its most fragile joint; it requires attribution the
houses accept as fair (`docs/economics/01`).

## Growth model

Supply first (verified houses with real demand), demand through supply (their audiences via
attributed links), then category demand (content, creators, campaigns) once the shelf is
deep enough to convert. Loops: house audience, cross-house, reviews, seller proof, content,
creators, CRM, referral (`docs/gtm/05`).

## Defensibility

The normalised catalogue, the verified-delivery review graph, seller workflow integration,
category reputation, the cross-house customer relationship (doc 01 §8). Not the code, not
the name, not being first (`docs/market/04`).

## Data advantage

Structured scent data with sources, transaction-gated reviews, attribution and ledger
evidence, cross-house behaviour — accumulated with minimal personal data by design
(`docs/data/`).

## Operational advantage

The platform owns the customer experience even when the house owns fulfilment (doc 06);
verification that cannot be skipped; reason codes everywhere; money that settles once with
two people approving; honest dashboards that show gaps as gaps.

## Risks

Borrowed demand that never becomes owned; disintermediation; weak sellers damaging trust;
thin margins on seller-originated orders; legal exposure on claims and designer references;
founder conflict without a signed agreement (doc 01 §9); and, from the audit, launching on
an unfixed money engine (`docs/architecture/04`).

## Milestones (evidence, not features)

1. Founders' agreement signed; entity path chosen. 2. Money engine correct and proven by
tests. 3. Three to five verified houses live with photographed catalogues. 4. Fifty
completed, attributed, correctly settled orders. 5. Cross-house behaviour observed and a
positive contribution path shown. 6. Decision: proceed, revise wedge, SaaS first, or pause
(doc 02 §11).
